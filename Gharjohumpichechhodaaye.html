A Multisensory Installation

A boy sneaks into the kitchen to find his spinning top.

His parents are in the other room, packing. Outside, something is happening — voices, movement, a radio saying things he doesn’t understand. But right now, there is the chulha his mother never lets him touch, and somewhere behind the dabbas, his lattu is hidden.

He has been waiting for this moment.

The Perspective Shift

The Partition of 1947 has been told through borders, massacres, political timelines, and the grief of a generation that survived it. Those stories are necessary. They are also told from a distance — the distance of adults who understood what was happening, who could name it, who carried the weight of knowing.

Ghar Jo Hum Piche Chhod Aaye is told from inside a moment a child did not understand.

The research uncovered what history books don’t linger on: children in 1947 who moved through one of the largest humanitarian crises in human history as children move through everything — curiously, without full comprehension, their attention on the things that mattered to them. There were already enough stories of gore, violence, and explicit grief. We were interested in something smaller and more devastating: the home in its final ordinary hour, seen through eyes that didn’t know it was the last time.

The inversion is the installation’s core. The visitor doesn’t enter an archive. They enter a kitchen.

The Architecture of Innocence

The Visual Scale

The space is built for a child’s body. Objects are stretched vertically — walls loom, furniture looms, the chulha is larger than expected. Eye level is set low. The visitor’s adult body is made to feel small in the space, because the child whose home this was felt the world as large and unthreatening, full of things just beyond reach.

The installation is also mapped precisely to a VR environment. Every physical object in the room occupies the same coordinates as its virtual counterpart. The visitor’s body and the child’s perspective inhabit the same point in space.

The Tactile & Interactive Elements

Two objects anchor the physical world: the chulha and the lattu.

The chulha has real fire. Not a simulation, not an orange light — fire, with its smell and warmth and the authority it carries in a kitchen. The mother’s prohibition on touching it gave it a particular weight in the child’s imagination. Now, with the parents in the other room and the world outside shifting into something none of them have words for, he can.

The lattu is hidden behind the dabbas. The visitor has to look for it — peer behind things, reach into spaces. The search is the interaction. Finding it is the joy. And when it is found, the visitor picks it up.

If they drop it on the floor, the installation ends.

The toy is left behind. The family has gone. The title finally makes complete sense.

The Auditory Landscape

The soundscape is layered and directional. In the kitchen: fire, the crackle of the chulha, the small domestic sounds of a home still functioning. From a window: the outside — rush, movement, the sound of something happening that has not yet entered the house. From the living room: a radio. Words. A voice saying something the child, playing in the kitchen, is not fully listening to.

The horror never arrives. It stays at the edges, in the other room, outside the window. The child is still inside. That is the design. The moment the violence enters the kitchen, the story becomes a different story — one that has already been told. This one ends before that.

The Emotional & Technical Hurdles

The Abstract Balance

The line between too subtle and too explicit was held by the object. The lattu carries no historical label. The chulha has no explanatory text beside it. Nothing in the installation tells the visitor what 1947 means or what Partition was. The weight comes from what the visitor brings in — the knowledge that something is wrong, held alongside the experience of a child who doesn’t know yet.

The story was kept singular and specific: one child, one kitchen, one hiding place, one toy. Specificity is what prevents abstraction from becoming emptiness. The visitor is not experiencing Partition in the general sense. They are experiencing the last hour of one boy’s home.

The Spatial Friction

The VR and physical mapping meant the visitor had to move in a particular way without being instructed to. The space had to feel explorable, not curated — casual, not ceremonial. A museum exhibit is walked through. This was designed to be moved through the way a child moves through a familiar room: with some aimlessness, some curiosity, attention going where it goes.

Handling the Weight

Using a child’s unknowing perspective on Partition is a choice that could read as evasion — as softening a history that shouldn’t be softened. The response to that critique is in the ending. The toy left on the floor is not soft. The installation doesn’t tell you that losing a home is sad. It makes you the one who dropped it.

The Silent Impact

The visitor leaves the space having played. Having touched fire. Having found a hidden toy. Having, in the final moment, left it behind.

The realization — that the casual exploration they just completed was a simulation of displacement, of a child’s last ordinary moment before everything changed — arrives after the door closes. That gap, between the experience and the understanding of it, is where the installation lives.

History records what happened. It rarely records how a kitchen smelled that morning, or what a child was looking for while his parents packed, or what it meant to leave a lattu on the floor.

Design that only records history keeps you at a distance from it. Design that puts you inside the fragile, human spaces history leaves out — that makes you drop the toy yourself — asks something different of you.

It asks you to remember a home you never lived in.
