A Satirical Game

You are a government minister. Documents land on your desk. You have two tools: a stamp and a paper cutter. Keep the corruption alive, and you keep your job. Let your conscience through, and the system suspends you. That is not the villain’s goal. That is yours.

The Satirical Hook

The win condition in Ministry of Misconduct is the inversion of every game you have ever played: do not be a hero. Manage the rot. Keep the system exactly as broken as it needs to be, because the moment you act ethically — the moment you let the right document through — the institution turns on you. Suspended. Removed. The system does not punish corruption. It punishes the threat of transparency.

This is not an exaggeration for effect. This is the observation the game is built on.

The targets are real: the scandals that never resolve, the politicians who emerge from inquiries untouched, the institutional machinery that exists not to deliver justice but to absorb it. In Ministry of Misconduct, politicians have extra lives — not as a power-up, but as a design truth. The system was built to protect them. The game just makes you operate that system with your own hands.

Why a game and not a documentary, an essay, an infographic? Because none of those make you do it. Satire observed is easy to dismiss. Satire played — satire that makes you enjoy the destruction — is something you can’t put down without looking at yourself first.

The Mechanics of Malice

The Balancing Act

The player manages nothing visible. There is no corruption meter on screen, no public trust gauge, no budget bar. The desk holds documents. The hands hold a stamp and a paper cutter. That is all the interface gives you.

What the player cannot see is the consequence architecture running underneath every choice. Every document stamped or destroyed shifts something — but not immediately. The system’s logic is opaque by design, because that is how real corruption operates. You don’t feel the damage as you do it. You feel it a few turns later, when the repercussion surfaces and you can’t quite trace it back to the moment you made it.

The Choices

Every document is one of two types: one that benefits the corrupt system, and one that threatens it. The player must decide — stamp it through, or feed it to the paper cutter.

Here is the seduction: the paper cutter is satisfying to use. The interaction is tactile, immediate, and fun in a way that the stamp isn’t. The game makes destruction feel good. You don’t choose corruption through reluctance. You slide into it through enjoyment — and by the time the consequences arrive, you have already built the habit.

That is the mechanic and the message at once.

The Visual/UI Tone

The desk is bureaucratic and bare. Two objects. A surface. Documents arriving. No music that energizes, no UI that rewards. The interface has the visual weight of an actual government office — dry, slightly soul-crushing, going through motions. The aesthetic doesn’t wink at you. It just sits there, the way the real system sits there, asking you to process the next file.

The Playtesting Hurdles & Friction

The Complexity Trap

The design challenge with a two-mechanic game is that simplicity can read as randomness. If stamp and cut feel arbitrary, the player is not experiencing moral weight — they are just guessing. The solution lives in the delayed consequence system: choices matter, but the gap between choice and repercussion teaches the player that they cannot optimize in real time. They have to develop a sense of the system. That sense, once developed, is exactly what a corrupt bureaucrat has: a feel for how far things can go before something breaks.

The game is still in development. The loop is being refined. But the core is sound — simple enough to understand in thirty seconds, deep enough to feel complicit in.

Defending the Satire

The response from peers and evaluators has not been “this is too dark.” It has been the opposite — genuine interest, recognition that this is something new. A game that makes you maintain a corrupt system is not a design anyone has seen before in this register. The cynicism doesn’t need defending because the premise lands before anyone has time to resist it. You sit down, you understand the rules, and something uncomfortable happens immediately.

The Verdict

The moment the game is trying to build toward is this: the player wins. They kept the system intact. Their score is high. Their minister is still employed.

And then they sit with what they just optimized.

The mirror the game holds up is not “corruption is bad” — that is a lesson nobody needs a game to teach them. The mirror is: you are good at this. You made the right calls. You knew which documents to destroy and which to let through, and you did it efficiently, and it felt fine.

That recognition — that the skills required to win this game are the skills required to maintain real corrupt systems — is the point the entire design is building toward.

Making a comfortable game about political ethics is easy and useless. Making a game that puts the player inside the machine, gives them satisfying tools, and rewards them for using those tools correctly — that is design doing something that essays and documentaries cannot. It makes you the subject of the critique, not the audience for it.

The Ministry is hiring. You are already qualified.
